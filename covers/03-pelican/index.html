Halcyon

A paperback modernism for a library of machine learning.

Halcyon takes its method from the post-war Penguin and Pelican paperbacks — Tschichold's horizontal grid, Marber's fixed title band, Facetti's abstract science covers — and applies it to a library about machines that learn.

Every cover is the same three bands: a paper band where the title is the hero, a black rule that carries the level and the length of the book, and a field of flat colour holding one abstract composition drawn from the book's own mechanism: a neuron, a causal-attention mask, a five-dimensional device mesh. The field's colour is the level, the way Penguin's green meant crime, so a shelf of Vizuara books sorts itself from across the room.

Everything is drawn in code with five inks, no raster images, and a publisher's device of its own: a kingfisher (the halcyon) in a capsule, because Vizuara teaches in capsules.

Device

A kingfisher — the halcyon — built from four shapes (a half-disc, a disc, two triangles) inside a capsule. It signs every cover at 44 px, small enough never to compete with the title.

  1. Imprint line. Device, "Vizuara Books", catalogue number — the signature, 13 px, same place on every book.
  2. Title band. Paper, 336 px deep. Title at 60–92 px, hand-broken; optional subtitle or series line. The same depth on every book, so titles hang from one line across the shelf.
  3. Level rule. A 36 px black band: level capsules and word on the left, capsules and hours on the right ("Forthcoming" for books not yet out).
  4. Emblem field. 720 × 516, flat level colour, one composition drawn from the subject in five inks. Compositions may bleed; nothing critical within 24 px of trim.
  5. Sub-series. Charlie and the Intelligence Factory keeps the grid and adds a shared set: factory roof, conveyor, and a volume numeral. The lit pane in the roof is the volume.
  6. Forthcoming. Unpublished books print as a proof: keylines only, one element inked in red.
