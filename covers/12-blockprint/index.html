छापthe impression a carved block leaves on cloth

Every Vizuara book is a length of block-printed cotton. Each title gets its own carved block, built from the actual mechanism of its subject — a causal mask, a pipeline schedule, an edge-detection kernel — and the block is stamped across the cloth into a field, the way an Ajrakh printer in Kutch builds a whole length from one carved unit. The metaphor is exact: stamping one block across a surface with a stride is a convolution, and the same block printed again and again is one model replicated across the data.

Level is the number of dye baths the cloth has been through — raw kora for beginner, madder for intermediate, indigo for advanced — and the four Charlie books are woven rather than printed: a double-ikat sub-series after the Patola of Patan, where every thread is dyed to a pre-computed grid, a bitmap made of thread.

Level = dye baths

Beginner — printed straight onto raw kora in indigo and iron black.
Intermediate — one bath in madder; the motif is reserved white, with iron black and indigo.
Advanced — through the indigo vat; reserved white, madder and iron black.

Type — both from Indian foundries

Eczar Bold, for titles Vaibhav Singh, Rosetta. A sharp display serif drawn alongside its Devanagari; the carved edge of its serifs sits well beside a wooden block. Anek Latin · imprint Ek Type, Mumbai. Variable width — set wide and tracked, like a mill stamp on the end of a bolt.
  1. FieldThe book's own carved block, stamped into a repeat by a seeded hand: each impression lands a little off register, each colour is a separate pass, and the ink thins between re-inkings.
  2. KinarOne border for the whole library — a black band with reserved lozenges, always at the same height. On a shelf of fifty it is the line that makes them one family.
  3. PalluThe end of the cloth, kept free of dye. The title is set here in Eczar, crisp against the printed field; it is the loudest thing on the cover.
  4. ChhaapThe printer's stamp: a carved square with a stepped V, and the imprint beside it. Level and length sit opposite, with a swatch of the dye bath.

The core six

One carved block per book, derived from the subject's own mechanism. Beneath each cover: the block itself, drawn flat before printing, as the carver would receive it.

Range

A one-word title and a three-line one; every level; a forthcoming book. The block changes, the cloth and the kinar never do.

Charlie and the Intelligence Factory

The four-book sub-series is woven, not printed. Its fields are double ikat after the Patola of Patan: each design is a naksha, a grid on graph paper to which every warp and weft thread is resist-dyed before weaving, so edges feather where tied bundles slip. Pomegranate yellow appears only here. The sub-series keeps the kinar, the pallu and the level colours of the main list.

Print wrap

Back, spine and front are one continuous length of cloth: the field runs around the spine, the kinar crosses all three at the same height. The spine carries the block's field at the top — a shelf of these reads as a stack of folded textiles — and the title below the kinar.

Where it comes from

Ajrakh of Kutch (Ajrakhpur and Dhamadka, Gujarat) and Sindh, printed by the Khatri community: a field built from one carved block, bounded by borders, with resist and mordant printed in separate registered passes before dyeing in indigo and madder. Dabu mud-resist of Bagru and Sanganeri printing (the Chhipa printers of Rajasthan) for the reserved, undyed ground. Patan Patola, the Salvi family's double ikat, for the Charlie sub-series. Blocks for all of these are traditionally carved in teak by specialist block-makers, many of them in Pethapur, Gujarat.

How it would be made for real

Commission each book's block from a block-carver working from these drawings, print a length with an Ajrakh workshop in natural dyes, and scan it for the edition — the procedural renders here become the specification. Name the carver and the printing workshop on the copyright page of every book, pay them per title, and do the same for a Patan weaving family if the Charlie series is ever woven.
